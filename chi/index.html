What Is Chi? A Systems Engineer’s Reframing

Chi is usually called a mysterious life energy. A systems engineer argues it is the flow of organization: a process, not a substance.

By Panagiotis Kokkorogiannis·10 October 2026·7 min read

In this article

  1. The problem with calling it “energy”
  2. What the sources actually describe
  3. Organization is a real thing, and it flows
  4. Where modern biology becomes relevant

Ask what chi is and you will get one of two answers, both unsatisfying.

The first comes from wellness culture: chi is life energy, a subtle force that flows through the body and can be blocked, unblocked, cultivated, or transmitted. The word energy does a great deal of work in this account, and none of it is precise.

The second comes from the skeptical direction: chi is nothing. A pre-scientific placeholder, a folk category that dissolved once we learned what actually moves through a body — ions, hormones, action potentials, oxygenated blood.

I spent fifteen years as an engineer before I spent any time on this question, and both answers struck me as premature. The first uses a word with a rigorous physical definition in a way that quietly abandons that definition. The second assumes that because a concept was described in the wrong vocabulary, there was nothing being described.

There is a third possibility, and it is the one this book argues for: chi is not a substance that flows. It is the flow of organization itself.

The problem with calling it “energy”

Energy is not a vague word. In physics it has a specific meaning, a unit, and a conservation law. It is the capacity to do work; it is measured in joules; and in a closed system, it is conserved.

If chi were a form of energy in that sense, it would be detectable. Energy always is. Every form we have identified — kinetic, thermal, chemical, electromagnetic, nuclear — announces itself through measurable interaction. A century of instrumentation sensitive enough to detect gravitational waves and single photons has not turned up a distinct vital force, and the absence is meaningful.

So one of two things is true. Either chi is a physical energy that has evaded every detector ever built, or the word energy is a mistranslation.

I think it is a mistranslation, and I think it happened in a specific and forgivable way. The classical Chinese texts describe something that behaves like a flow, that can be strong or depleted, that moves through the body along paths, and that correlates with health and vitality. When these texts reached European readers, the closest available concept in nineteenth-century physics was energy. The translation stuck. It has been distorting the conversation ever since.

What the sources actually describe

This matters because when you go back to the primary texts, the energy reading is not the most natural one.

The Neiye — an inner-cultivation text from roughly the fourth century BCE, and probably the oldest Chinese writing on the subject — describes qi in terms of ordering and alignment. What produces vitality is not the accumulation of a fluid but the achievement of a condition: stillness, regularity, a body and mind arranged so that processes run without conflict. Qi in the Neiye is closer to coherence than to fuel.

Mencius adds something stranger and more interesting. His haoran zhi qi, usually rendered “flood-like qi,” is explicitly tied to moral conduct. It accumulates through right action and dissipates through wrongdoing. Whatever this is, it is not a metabolic quantity. No substance behaves this way. But an organizational state — a pattern of integration between what a person does, intends, and is — might.

The Huangdi Neijing, the foundational medical text, maps channels through the body and describes qi moving along them. This is the passage most often read as anatomical, and it is where the modern controversy lives. But read as a functional map — a description of how influence propagates through a system, which regions are coupled to which — it is doing something recognizable. Engineers draw diagrams like this constantly. They are not pictures of pipes. They are pictures of dependency.

I go through these texts in detail in the Sources Library, including translated passages, because the argument depends on what they actually say rather than on what they are popularly assumed to say.

Organization is a real thing, and it flows

Here is the move that makes the reframing work.

Living systems are not closed. They are open systems held far from thermodynamic equilibrium by a continuous throughput of energy and matter. Ilya Prigogine showed that systems in this condition can spontaneously generate and maintain structure — his dissipative structures. A candle flame, a convection cell, a hurricane, a cell, an organism, an ecosystem: all of them are patterns that persist not despite the flow through them but because of it.

The crucial point is that the pattern is not the material. The atoms in a flame are replaced continuously. The molecules in your body are almost entirely different from the ones you had a decade ago. What persists is the organization — the specific arrangement of relations and constraints that keeps the process running.

That organization is not a substance. It also is not nothing. It has structure, it can be more or less coherent, it can degrade, it can be maintained through effort, and it propagates through a system in ways that can be traced. It behaves, in short, very much like the thing the classical texts were pointing at.

This is what I mean by the flow of organization. Not a mystical field. Not an undetected particle. The dynamic architecture of a system that is holding itself together against entropy — and the ways that architecture strengthens, weakens, and moves.

Where modern biology becomes relevant

If organization propagates through living tissue, we should be able to find mechanisms that do the propagating. We can, and this is where the reframing earns its keep rather than merely sounding elegant.

Michael Levin’s work on bioelectricity has shown that cells communicate through voltage gradients across tissue, and that these gradients carry morphological information — instructions about what structure to build, not merely metabolic signals. Manipulate the bioelectric pattern and you can change what an organism grows, independent of its genome. Tissue stores and routes structural information electrically.

I want to be careful here, because this is exactly the point where books like mine usually overreach.

Levin’s research does not validate meridian theory. It does not show that acupuncture channels exist as anatomical structures. What it shows is that a category the classical texts were reaching for — information about form and function, propagating through tissue along routes, capable of being modulated — is not a fantasy. It is an active research area with a growing body of results.

That is a much weaker claim than “science has confirmed chi.” It is also a much more defensible one, and I would rather make defensible claims.

I unpack the bioelectric research and its limits in Michael Levin’s Bioelectricity, Explained for Non-Biologists, and the specific question of acupuncture and neurophysiology in a separate piece, because it deserves more care than a paragraph.

What this reframing costs

Every reframing gives something up. This one gives up the most commercially valuable feature of the traditional account: the claim that chi can be transmitted from one person to another, projected, or manipulated at a distance.

If chi is the organizational state of a dissipative system, it cannot be handed over. Organization is not portable. You can influence a system’s organization — through movement, breath, attention, sleep, diet, physical intervention — but you influence it by acting on the system, not by donating a quantity to it. There is no reservoir to draw from.

This rules out a large amount of what is currently sold under the heading of energy work. I consider that a feature. A concept that explains everything and forbids nothing is not doing intellectual work.

It also rules out something I would personally have liked to keep. The idea that individual consciousness persists after death is sometimes defended by appeal to the First Law of Thermodynamics: energy is conserved, consciousness is energy, therefore consciousness is conserved. The argument does not survive contact with the definitions. Energy is conserved; the arrangement of energy is not. When a system dissipates, its organization is genuinely lost even though every joule is accounted for. Under the reframing I am proposing, this is exactly what death is.

I make that argument in full in the book, and I make it against my own preference.

The boundary

Where the boundary sits

I am an engineer, not a scientist. The distinction matters, and I want to be explicit about it because the rest of this site rests on it.

What is established: dissipative structures are real physics. Bioelectric signaling in morphogenesis is real biology. Information has a thermodynamic cost. Complex adaptive systems exhibit properties their components lack.

What is my interpretation: that these separate findings can be read as describing a single phenomenon at different scales, and that the classical vocabulary of qi, prana, and pneuma was mapping the same phenomenon from the inside, phenomenologically, without instruments.

That second claim is not a scientific result. It is a philosophical synthesis, and it should be judged as one — on coherence, on explanatory reach, on whether it survives contact with the evidence it invokes. Not on whether a laboratory has confirmed it, because no laboratory has been asked the question in this form.

The convergence between ancient traditions and modern systems science is, I think, real and worth taking seriously. But it is convergence of description, not vindication of doctrine. Two maps of the same territory drawn with different instruments will resemble each other. That resemblance is evidence about the territory. It is not evidence that either mapmaker was doing physics.

This is the conceptual spine of The Learning Earth. The book extends the same reframing upward — from organisms to ecosystems to the planet itself — and asks what follows if organization, rather than energy or matter, is the thing that accumulates.
